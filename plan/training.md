# Training Plan

**Goal for the next 12 weeks:** lose fat, keep and build muscle, and turn "every 3 days" from an intention into a habit.
**Start date:** Friday, October 2, 2026 (Session A)

---

## 1. What changed from your current routine, and why

Your current routine came from *Powerfully Fit*, and it is a solid base. You already train every major muscle group with good exercises. I kept almost all of them. What changed is how they are arranged, plus a few additions.

**One long session became two shorter ones.** Right now you do 11 exercises at 4 sets each. That is 44 working sets, which takes roughly 75 to 90 minutes with rest. A workout that long is easy to put off, and that is probably a big reason "every 3 days" turns into "every 10 days." The new plan alternates two full-body sessions, **A** and **B**. Each has 7 exercises at 3 sets and takes about 50 minutes including warm-up. A shorter workout is much easier to show up for.

**Both sessions are full-body on purpose.** I could have split the routine into "push day" and "pull day." Then if you missed a session, a muscle group might go two weeks without training. With two full-body sessions, every visit works legs, chest, back, shoulders, arms, and core. If life gets in the way and you only make it once in a week, you still trained everything.

**Three hard sets instead of four moderate ones.** Four sets of 11 at the same weight usually means the early sets are too easy and the last one is a grind. The new plan uses a rep range (for example, 8 to 12) and asks you to push each set close to your limit. The effort level is explained in Section 3. Three sets done this way build as much muscle as four sets done comfortably, in less time.

**Added: leg press.** Leg extensions and leg curls are good, but they each train one joint. To build leaner, stronger legs at our age, you want at least one multi-joint lower-body lift. The leg press lets you work the whole leg hard while you control the depth. That matters for your knee. Unlike squats, there is no balance demand or spinal load.

**Added: core work every session.** You said you do no ab work right now, so each session ends with about 5 minutes of it. Session A uses a weighted crunch-type movement. Session B uses a stability circuit (plank, side plank, dead bug). The stability work protects your lower back on the bike, on the pickleball court, and during deadlifts.

**Swapped: upright rows → face pulls.** Upright rows pull the arms up while the shoulders are rotated inward. Over time that can pinch the tendons at the top of the shoulder, and the risk climbs after 50. Face pulls train the rear shoulders and the small rotator cuff muscles that keep the joint healthy. They also balance all the pressing you do. Lateral raises stay in, so your side delts still get direct work. If you really miss upright rows, you can do them with a wide grip, raising the bar only to lower-chest height. Treat that as an option, not the default.

**Added: yoga, twice a week, short.** You burned out doing it nearly every day, so this plan asks for two 20- to 25-minute sessions a week. Both flows are written out in Section 7. They are aimed at what your body needs from yoga right now: hips and hamstrings after riding, plus mobility, balance, and back health. Doing more is fine when you feel like it, but two sessions count as success.

## 2. The weekly rhythm

You lift **every third day**, alternating A and B. The other activities fit in the two days between gym sessions.

| Day in the cycle | What it is for |
|---|---|
| **Day 1 – Gym** | Session A or B (about 50 minutes) |
| **Day 2 – Off the gym** | Pickleball, a walk, an easy road ride, or yoga |
| **Day 3 – Off the gym** | Mountain bike ride (when it lands here), a walk, yoga, or rest |

**The mountain bike rule:** ride on a **weekday**, on the **second** day after a gym session, which is the day right before your next one. That way you are never riding the day after a hard leg session, and your legs are fresh for the climbing. Weekends are out because the trails are crowded and the parking lots are full. Because gym days fall three days apart, every week has two or three "day before a gym day" slots, and at least one of them is always a weekday. Pick that one. If you are wiped out after a big ride, do the next gym session anyway, but lighten the leg exercises (one fewer set, or a bit less weight).

Because a 3-day cycle doesn't line up with a 7-day week, your gym days move around the calendar. The tracker handles that, so you don't have to work it out yourself. Just remember "two days off, then lift."

### What to do when you miss a day

You run your own business, and some days will blow up. These rules keep one missed day from turning into ten:

1. **Missed a gym day? Go the next day.** Then count your two off days from that new date. The cycle shifts by one day, and that's fine.
2. **Short on time? Do the Express Session** (Section 6). It takes 30 minutes and covers the essentials. A short workout beats a skipped one by a mile.
3. **Never miss twice in a row.** One missed day is life. Two in a row is the start of a new habit, and not the one you want.
4. **Don't double up.** Never cram two sessions into one day or do gym days back to back to "catch up." Just pick the cycle back up.

## 3. How hard to push, and how to progress

### Effort: "one or two reps left in the tank"

For every working set, pick a weight you can lift within the rep range. Stop when you could do **one or two more good reps, but not three**. Trainers call this "1 to 2 reps in reserve." The last rep should be slow and take real effort, but your form should still be clean. You are not grinding to total failure, which is hard on joints and recovery at our age. You also aren't stopping while the set still feels easy.

A useful check: if the last two reps move as fast as the first two, the weight is too light.

### Progression: the "double progression" method

Every exercise has a rep range, such as 8 to 12. You progress in two steps:

1. **Add reps first.** Keep the same weight and try to get one more rep on at least one set each time you repeat the session.
2. **Then add weight.** Once you hit the **top of the range on all 3 sets** with good form, increase the weight by the smallest step available next time. Your reps will drop back toward the bottom of the range, and you build them back up.

**Worked example (bench press, 8–12 reps):**

| Session | Weight | Set 1 | Set 2 | Set 3 | What happens next |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Oct 2 | 80 lb | 12 | 11 | 10 | Stay at 80 and try for more reps |
| Oct 8 | 80 lb | 12 | 12 | 11 | Close. Stay at 80 |
| Oct 14 | 80 lb | 12 | 12 | 12 | All sets at 12, so go up next time |
| Oct 20 | 85 lb | 10 | 9 | 9 | New weight. Build back toward 12 |

**Smallest weight jumps:**

- Barbell: +5 lb (2.5 on each side)
- Dumbbells: the next dumbbell up (usually +5 lb per hand)
- Machines and cables: one pin down (usually +5 to +10 lb)
- Leg press: +10 to +20 lb

For small muscles like biceps, side delts, and triceps, progress will be slow. Moving from 15-lb to 20-lb lateral raises is a 33% jump. If you hit 15 reps on all sets and can't manage 8 good reps at the next weight, stay put and slow the lowering phase down instead.

### Calibrating in your first two sessions

Your current weights are the starting points, but you've been doing them for 4 sets of 11 at a moderate effort. In your first A and first B session, treat the first set of each exercise as a test:

- If you could easily have done **15 or more** reps, add weight for set 2.
- If you **couldn't reach 8** reps, drop the weight a little.
- Write down where you end up. That becomes your real starting weight.

Exercises that are new to you (leg press, face pulls, ab work) should start **light**. Spend the first two sessions learning the movement, then start pushing.

### Tempo and form

On every rep, **lift with control and lower slowly, over about 2 to 3 seconds.** The lowering half of the rep builds a lot of muscle and is where most injuries happen when people rush. No bouncing, no swinging, and no heaving with your back.

## 4. Warm-up (8 minutes, before every session)

Skipping the warm-up is the fastest way for a 60-year-old body to get hurt, so treat it as part of the workout, not optional.

1. **5 minutes on the stationary bike or elliptical, easy pace.** The bike is the knee-friendly choice. You should be breathing a bit harder but still able to talk.
2. **Bodyweight sit-to-stands × 10.** Stand in front of a bench, sit back until you lightly touch it, then stand up. This wakes up your hips and knees through a range you control.
3. **Hip hinges × 10.** With your hands on your hips, push your hips back and tip forward with a flat back until you feel a stretch in your hamstrings, then stand tall. This rehearses the deadlift pattern.
4. **Arm circles × 10 each direction**, then **band pull-aparts × 15** (or light cable face pulls × 15) to warm up your shoulders.
5. **Ramp-up sets on your first leg exercise and first upper-body exercise.** Do one set of 10 reps at about half your working weight, then one set of 5 reps at about three-quarters. After that, start your working sets.

## 5. The sessions

Rest times are guidelines. Rest longer on big lifts if you need to, but don't let a rest stretch to 5 minutes while you check your phone. The sessions are designed to fit in about 50 minutes.

### SESSION A — Leg Press, Bench, Pulldown

| # | Exercise | Sets × Reps | Starting weight | Rest |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Leg press | 3 × 10–12 | Find it (see notes) | 2 min |
| 2 | Barbell bench press | 3 × 8–12 | 80 lb | 2 min |
| 3 | Lat pulldown | 3 × 8–12 | 80 lb | 90 sec |
| 4 | Leg curl (seated or lying) | 3 × 10–12 | 70 lb | 90 sec |
| 5 | Dumbbell side lateral raise | 3 × 12–15 | 15 lb each | 60 sec |
| 6 | Triceps rope pushdown | 3 × 10–15 | 45 lb | 60 sec |
| 7 | Cable crunch **or** captain's chair knee raise | 3 × 12–15 | Light | 60 sec |

**Time-saver:** do exercises 5 and 6 as a superset. Do a set of lateral raises, go straight to a set of pushdowns, then rest 60 seconds and repeat. That saves about 5 minutes.

**1. Leg press.** Sit with your back and hips flat against the pad and your feet shoulder-width apart, in the middle to upper part of the platform. Placing your feet higher shifts work toward your hips and away from your knees, which is what you want with your knee. Lower the sled until your knees bend to about 90 degrees, or as far as feels comfortable. Stop before your lower back starts to curl off the pad. Press through your whole foot, mostly heels and mid-foot, back to the top, without snapping your knees into a locked position. **Starting weight:** machines vary a lot. On a plate-loaded sled, start with one 45-lb plate per side. On a pin-select machine, start around 100 to 120 lb. Add weight each set until you find a weight that makes 12 reps hard. Expect to end up well above your leg extension weight. **Knee check:** you should feel your thighs and glutes working, not a sharp or pinching feeling in the knee. If the knee complains, shorten the depth.

**2. Barbell bench press.** Lie down with your eyes under the bar and your feet flat on the floor. Pinch your shoulder blades together and down, as if tucking them into your back pockets, and keep them there the whole set. That protects your shoulders. Lower the bar under control to the lower part of your chest, with your elbows about 45 to 60 degrees out from your body rather than flared straight out to the sides. Press back up toward your face slightly. **Safety:** use the safety arms or a spotter when you push close to your limit.

**3. Lat pulldown.** Grip the bar a little wider than your shoulders, palms facing away. Sit tall with the thigh pad snug, lean back just slightly, and pull the bar to your upper chest by driving your elbows down toward your back pockets. Think "elbows down," not "hands down," so your back does the work instead of your arms. Let the bar rise slowly until your arms are straight and you feel a stretch in your lats. Never pull the bar behind your neck.

**4. Leg curl.** Line your knees up with the machine's pivot point. The ankle pad should rest just above your heels. Curl the pad toward you and squeeze your hamstrings hard for a moment at the top, then lower over 3 seconds. On the lying version, keep your hips pressed into the pad. If they rise, the weight is too heavy.

**5. Dumbbell side lateral raise.** Stand tall with a slight forward lean at the hips and a soft bend in your elbows. Raise the dumbbells out to your sides and lead with your elbows, stopping at shoulder height. Keep your thumbs level or slightly up, not tipped down like pouring a pitcher. The "pouring" version pinches the shoulder. Lower slowly. This is an exercise where lighter and stricter beats heavier and swingier. If you're shrugging or swinging to get the weight up, go lighter.

**6. Triceps rope pushdown.** Stand facing the cable with a rope attachment at the top. Pin your elbows to your sides and keep them there. Push the rope down until your arms are straight, spreading the rope ends apart at the bottom, and squeeze the backs of your arms. Let the rope rise slowly until your forearms are just past parallel with the floor. Only your forearms should move.

**7a. Cable crunch.** Kneel facing a high cable with a rope attachment. Hold the rope ends beside your head and keep your hips still. Crunch down by curling your ribcage toward your hips, rounding your spine, and exhaling hard as you go. Return slowly. Don't sit back on your heels or pull with your arms; the movement comes from your abs. Start around 40 to 50 lb.
**7b. Captain's chair knee raise (alternative).** Support yourself on your forearms with your back flat against the pad. Raise your knees toward your chest, and at the top, curl your pelvis up slightly. That curl is what works the lower abs. Lower slowly without swinging. If 12 is too many at first, do what you can with good form and build up.

### SESSION B — Deadlift, Incline, Row

| # | Exercise | Sets × Reps | Starting weight | Rest |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Romanian (stiff-legged) deadlift | 3 × 8–12 | 50 lb (expect to increase quickly) | 2 min |
| 2 | Incline dumbbell press | 3 × 8–12 | 40 lb each | 2 min |
| 3 | Seated cable row | 3 × 8–12 | 80 lb | 90 sec |
| 4 | Leg extension | 3 × 10–15 | 110 lb | 90 sec |
| 5 | Face pull | 3 × 12–15 | 25–30 lb | 60 sec |
| 6 | Dumbbell curl | 3 × 8–12 | 25 lb each | 60 sec |
| 7 | Core circuit (below) | 2 rounds | Bodyweight | 60 sec between rounds |

**Time-saver:** superset exercises 5 and 6, the same way as in Session A.

**1. Romanian deadlift.** This is a stiff-legged deadlift done the safer way, with a slight bend in your knees. Stand tall holding the bar or dumbbells against your thighs. Keeping your knees softly bent and your back flat, push your hips straight back like you're closing a car door with your rear end. The weight slides down your thighs. Lower until you feel a strong stretch in your hamstrings, usually somewhere between just below the knee and mid-shin. Then squeeze your glutes and drive your hips forward to stand. Your back should never round. If it does, you've gone too low. **On weight:** 50 lb is almost certainly light for you. This lift trains the biggest muscles in your body: hamstrings, glutes, and back. Add weight every session as long as your form stays clean, until 12 reps is genuinely hard. It's realistic to be lifting 95 to 135 lb here within a couple of months.

**2. Incline dumbbell press.** Set the bench to a low incline, about 30 degrees, or the second or third notch. Kick the dumbbells up to your shoulders one at a time from your knees. With your shoulder blades pinched back like on the bench press, lower the dumbbells to the sides of your upper chest with your elbows angled about 45 degrees. Press up and slightly in, without banging the dumbbells together. When you finish, bring the dumbbells to your chest and sit up using your legs, rather than dropping them out wide.

**3. Seated cable row.** Sit tall with your feet on the platform and knees slightly bent. Pull the handle toward your lower ribs, squeezing your shoulder blades together at the end. Hold for one second. Let the weight pull your arms forward slowly until they're straight and you feel your upper back stretch. Keep your torso mostly upright. A little lean is fine, but rocking back and forth means the weight is too heavy.

**4. Leg extension.** Adjust the seat so your knee lines up with the machine's pivot point, with the pad on your lower shin just above the ankle. Raise the pad until your legs are straight, squeeze your quads for a moment at the top, then lower over 3 seconds. **Knee note:** leg extensions put a fair amount of stress on the knee, especially at the bottom of the movement. If your knee complains, shorten the range so you only lower about two-thirds of the way down, or drop the weight. This exercise moved to a 10–15 rep range for the same reason. A slightly lighter weight for more reps is easier on the joint.

**5. Face pull.** Set a cable with a rope attachment at upper-chest to face height. Grip the rope with your thumbs pointing back toward you. Pull the rope toward your forehead, driving your elbows high and wide and pulling the rope ends apart, so you end in a "double biceps" pose. Squeeze the backs of your shoulders, then return slowly. Go light. This is a joint-health exercise, not a strength test.

**6. Dumbbell curl.** Stand tall with your elbows at your sides. Curl the dumbbells up, turning your palms to face the ceiling as you lift, and squeeze at the top. Lower slowly over about 3 seconds. Your elbows shouldn't drift forward, and your body shouldn't rock. If you have to swing, the weight is too heavy.

**7. Core circuit.** Do these back to back, rest 60 seconds, then repeat once more:
- **Dead bug × 8 per side.** Lie on your back with your arms pointing at the ceiling and your knees bent at 90 degrees over your hips. Press your lower back firmly into the floor. Slowly lower your right arm overhead and straighten your left leg toward the floor, exhaling as you go, without letting your back arch. Return, and switch sides. This exercise is slow and controlled, and harder than it looks.
- **Side plank × 20–30 seconds per side.** Prop yourself on one forearm with your elbow under your shoulder, knees bent and stacked (the easier version) or legs straight. Lift your hips so your body forms a straight line. Don't let your hips sag.
- **Front plank × 30–45 seconds.** Forearms on the floor and body straight from head to heels. Squeeze your glutes, pull your ribs down toward your hips, and breathe. When 45 seconds feels easy, make it harder by squeezing everything tighter, not by holding longer.

## 7. Yoga

Two sessions a week, 20 to 25 minutes each. Do them on off-days or right after a ride. You can follow along with a free video if you'd like more variety, but these two flows are built for your needs. Breathe slowly through your nose, move gently into each pose, and never force a stretch.

**Knee care in yoga:** put a folded towel or blanket under your knee whenever it touches the floor. Skip deep kneeling poses like Hero pose if they bother you. If a pose twists or loads your knee in a way that feels pinchy, back off, or use the alternative listed.

## 8. Your outdoor days

**Mountain bike ride (once a week, 10–15 miles, about 1,500 ft).** This is the anchor of your week. It's your longest cardio session, it builds leg endurance, and it's the activity you enjoy most, so protect it. A few tips:

- **Fuel it.** Eat a carb-heavy breakfast 1.5 to 2 hours before you ride (see the nutrition plan for an example). For rides longer than about 90 minutes, eat something small every 45 to 60 minutes, like a banana, a couple of fig bars, or an energy chew. That keeps your energy up on the climbs and stops you from raiding the fridge afterward.
- **Hydrate.** Bring water. On warm days, add an electrolyte mix.
- **Recover.** Within an hour of finishing, have a meal or snack with protein and carbs, like chocolate milk or the chicken-and-rice bowl.
- **Knee note.** Knee pain on a bike is often caused by saddle height. A saddle that's too low tends to strain the front of the knee, and one that's too high strains the back of the knee. At the bottom of your pedal stroke, your knee should still have a slight bend of about 25 to 35 degrees. If your knee aches after rides, this is the first thing to check. A bike shop can do a quick fitting.

**Pickleball (1–2 times a week, about 2 hours).** Two hours of pickleball is genuine interval training, with lots of quick starts, stops, and side-to-side movement. Warm up for 5 to 10 minutes first with brisk walking, side shuffles, and easy dinking. Wear court shoes with lateral support, not running shoes, which are built for forward motion and can let your ankle roll. If your knee feels tender on the court, a simple compression sleeve often helps.

**Walks (2 miles) and road rides (the Lafayette loop).** The Lafayette loop is a set of road routes of different lengths, the most common being about 11 miles. These are easy "recovery cardio" days. Keep the pace comfortable enough to talk in full sentences. That easy effort is great for heart health and fat burning, and it doesn't take anything away from your lifting.

**Hiking:** your knee doesn't like hikes over 6 miles, so keep mountain hikes under that, or use trekking poles, which take a meaningful amount of load off the knees on downhills.

## 9. The 12-week arc

| Weeks | Focus |
|---|---|
| **1–2** (Oct 2 – Oct 15) | **Learn and calibrate.** Get the new routine down, find your real working weights, and nail your form on the new exercises. Leave 2 to 3 reps in the tank. The win here is showing up every third day. |
| **3–6** (Oct 16 – Nov 12) | **Build.** Push to 1 to 2 reps in reserve. Use double progression on every lift. |
| **7** (Nov 13 – Nov 19) | **Lighter week.** Do 2 sets instead of 3 at the same weights. Keep riding and playing pickleball. A planned easy week lets your joints and connective tissue catch up, which matters more at 60 than at 30. You'll come back stronger. |
| **8–11** (Nov 20 – Dec 17) | **Build again.** If you've been consistent, add a 4th set to the first two exercises in each session. |
| **12** (Dec 18 – Dec 24) | **Lighter week and check-in.** Take measurements, compare your lifts to Week 1, and decide what to adjust for the next block. A good time for follow-up blood work. |

## 10. Recovery

- **Sleep 7 to 8 hours.** Your muscles grow while you sleep, not while you lift. Poor sleep also increases hunger and cravings the next day.
- **Protein at every meal.** Covered in the nutrition plan. At our age, muscles need a bigger dose of protein per meal (about 35 to 45 g) to get the same building signal.
- **Listen to joint pain versus muscle soreness.** Muscle soreness, the dull and achy kind, a day or two after training is normal. Sharp, pinching, or joint pain during an exercise means stop that exercise, and we'll find a substitute.
- **Creatine (optional).** Creatine monohydrate, 3 to 5 grams a day, is one of the best-studied supplements for building muscle and strength in older adults. It's inexpensive and has a long safety record. Run it by your doctor first.
