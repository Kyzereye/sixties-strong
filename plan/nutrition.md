# Nutrition Plan

**Goals:** lose fat steadily, keep and build muscle, and bring your cholesterol down, all without giving up the foods you like or spending more money.

> This plan is general guidance, not medical advice. When your blood work comes in, we'll adjust it. Please share this plan and your results with your doctor, especially if medication comes up.

---

## 1. Your daily targets

| Target | Amount | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Calories** | **About 2,100 on normal days, about 2,500 on big ride or long pickleball days** | A modest deficit that takes off fat without costing you muscle or energy |
| **Protein** | **150 g or more** (about 35–45 g per meal) | Builds and protects muscle while you lose fat |
| **Fiber** | **30 g or more** | Soluble fiber directly lowers LDL cholesterol and keeps you full |
| **Saturated fat** | **Under 15 g** | The biggest dietary driver of high LDL cholesterol |
| **Sodium** | **Under about 2,300 mg** | Your blood pressure is good, so this is about keeping it that way. Canned soup and deli meat are where sodium hides. |
| **Water** | About 8–10 cups, plus more on ride days | Hydration, energy, and appetite control |

### Where the calorie number comes from

At 5'9" (69 inches), 180 lb, and 59½ years old, your body burns about **1,620 calories a day at complete rest**. That's your basal metabolic rate, calculated with the Mifflin-St Jeor equation, the standard formula dietitians use. Your activity (lifting every third day, a weekly mountain bike ride, pickleball, walks, and road rides) multiplies that by about 1.5. That puts your average daily burn at **about 2,400 to 2,600 calories**, with mountain bike days well above that. Eating about 2,100 creates a deficit of roughly 300 to 500 calories a day. That should take off **about half a pound to a pound a week**. It's slow enough that most of what you lose is fat, not muscle, and you won't feel starved or lose strength in the gym.

These numbers are estimates, not facts. Your logs will tell us what's actually happening. After two to three weeks, we compare your weekly average weight against what you're eating and adjust:

- **Weight not moving after 3 weeks:** cut about 150 calories a day.
- **Losing more than 1.5 lb a week, or your lifts are dropping:** add about 150 calories a day.

### Why so much protein

When you're losing weight, your body draws on both fat and muscle unless it has a reason to keep the muscle. Lifting gives it that reason, and protein supplies the building material. Older muscles are also less responsive to protein than younger ones. Researchers call it "anabolic resistance." So a 60-year-old needs a **bigger dose per meal**, about 35 to 45 g, to get the same muscle-building signal a 25-year-old gets from 20 g. That's why every meal in this plan is built around a solid protein portion rather than saving it all for dinner.

For reference, 35 to 45 g of protein looks like one of these:

- 5–6 oz of cooked chicken, turkey, fish, or lean beef
- 1 cup of cottage cheese plus 1 cup of Greek yogurt
- 1 whole egg plus ¾ cup egg whites plus ½ cup black beans
- A scoop of protein powder in a glass of milk, plus something else

## 2. How this plan lowers your cholesterol

Food can move your cholesterol numbers meaningfully, often by 10 to 20 percent or more when several changes are combined. These are the levers, roughly in order of impact:

1. **Cut saturated fat.** Saturated fat raises LDL more than anything else you eat. The main sources in your current diet are cheese, mayo, deli ham, fattier beef, ice cream, and fast food. You don't have to eliminate them, just shrink them and swap smartly (see the swaps below).
2. **Eat more soluble fiber.** Oats, beans, lentils, barley, apples, citrus, and berries contain a gel-like fiber that binds cholesterol in your gut and carries it out of your body. Aim for at least 10 g of soluble fiber a day, which comes naturally if you eat oats or beans most days. **Beans are already one of your favorite foods, and they're one of the most powerful cholesterol-lowering foods there is.**
3. **Lose some body fat.** Losing even 10 to 15 lb typically lowers LDL and triglycerides and raises HDL.
4. **Exercise.** Your riding, pickleball, and lifting help raise HDL, the "good" cholesterol that clears the bad kind out.
5. **Add healthy fats.** Replacing butter and cheese with olive oil, nuts, and avocado lowers LDL. Fatty fish like salmon, trout, sardines, and tuna, eaten twice a week, mainly lowers triglycerides.
6. **Go easy on alcohol and sugar.** Both raise triglycerides. Cutting soda is already a big win here.

**An easy extra: psyllium fiber.** Plain psyllium husk powder, the main ingredient in Metamucil (buy an unflavored, sugar-free version), is pure soluble fiber. One rounded teaspoon to one tablespoon a day, stirred into water or your overnight oats, has been shown to lower LDL by several percent on its own. It's cheap and simple. Start with a small amount and drink plenty of water with it. Ask your doctor if you take other medications, since it can slow how some of them are absorbed.

**About eggs:** for most people, the cholesterol in eggs affects blood cholesterol much less than saturated fat does. Until we see your numbers, the plan uses about 1 whole egg a day, plus egg whites when you want more. If your labs look fine, you can eat more whole eggs.

## 3. Upgrading your current staples

You don't need a new diet. You need small upgrades to the one you have.

| What you eat now | The upgrade | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| White rice | **Brown rice**, or half brown and half white if you're easing in | About 3 times the fiber. Microwave pouches make it just as easy. |
| Chicken | Keep it. **Skinless breast, or thighs with the fat trimmed.** Cook in olive oil, not butter. | Chicken skin is where most of the saturated fat is. |
| Corn and peas | Keep them, but **also add a green vegetable** (broccoli, green beans, spinach) most nights | Corn and peas are starchy. Greens add volume and fiber for very few calories. |
| Deli ham | **Low-sodium turkey breast**, or sliced leftover chicken | Ham is processed red meat with a lot of sodium. Turkey breast is leaner, and leftover chicken is better still. |
| Regular cheese slice | **Reduced-fat Swiss or provolone**, one slice | A regular slice of cheese has about 5 g of saturated fat, a third of your daily limit. Reduced-fat has about 2 g. |
| Mayo | **Light mayo** (1 tablespoon), or mustard alone, or mashed avocado | Regular mayo is about 100 calories a tablespoon. Light is about 45. |
| White bread | **100% whole-wheat bread.** The first ingredient should say "whole wheat flour," with 3 g or more of fiber per slice. | More fiber, more protein, keeps you full longer |
| Canned soup | **Homemade lentil soup** (Recipe 2), or the canned-soup rules below | Canned soup is cheap, but it's usually loaded with sodium and short on protein. |
| White pasta | **Whole-wheat or chickpea pasta**, 2 oz dry per serving, with **marinara** rather than cream sauces | More fiber and protein. Cream sauces like Alfredo are saturated-fat bombs. |
| Beef | **Lean cuts: 93% lean ground beef, sirloin, eye of round, flank steak.** About 2 times a week. | Same beef taste, a fraction of the saturated fat of 80/20 ground beef, ribeye, or brisket |
| Beans | **More of them.** Rinse canned beans in a colander. | Rinsing removes about 40% of the sodium. Beans are cheap, high in fiber, and high in protein. |
| Pickle with a sandwich | Keep it if you like. **Just be aware** that one spear has about 300 mg of sodium. | It's not a problem, just something to know on a day you also had canned soup. |
| Tomato with a sandwich | Keep it, and add lettuce, spinach, or onion | More vegetables, more crunch, almost no calories |

### The upgraded sandwich

This is your current sandwich, rebuilt:

- 2 slices 100% whole-wheat bread
- 4 oz low-sodium turkey breast (about double the meat most people use, which is where the protein comes from)
- 1 slice reduced-fat Swiss or provolone
- Mustard on one slice, 1 tablespoon light mayo on the other
- Tomato slices, lettuce or spinach, and a pickle if you want it

**About 435 calories, 42 g protein, 7 g fiber, 3.5 g saturated fat.** The version with white bread, 2 oz of ham, a regular cheese slice, and regular mayo comes to about 420 calories, but only 20 g protein, 2 g fiber, and 7 g of saturated fat. The calories are about the same, but the upgraded sandwich has twice the protein and half the saturated fat.

### Rules for canned soup

When you do grab a can, it's still cheap and easy, and that's fine. Use these rules:

1. **Choose bean, lentil, split pea, minestrone, or chicken-and-vegetable soups.** Skip anything "cream of," chowders, and broccoli cheddar. Those are high in saturated fat.
2. **Buy "low sodium" or "reduced sodium."** Look for 480 mg or less per serving. Most cans hold two servings, so check that too.
3. **Add protein.** Canned soup alone usually has only 10 to 15 g of protein per can. Stir in 4 oz of leftover chicken, a can of rinsed beans, or eat it with a side of cottage cheese or Greek yogurt.
4. **Add vegetables.** A handful of frozen mixed vegetables or spinach costs pennies and makes it more filling.

## 5. Beer and ice cream

These stay in the plan. A plan that bans the foods you enjoy is a plan you'll quit. The goal is to make them **planned, portioned, and logged** rather than automatic.

### Beer

- **Budget:** about 3 to 4 beers a week, and no more than 2 in a sitting. Ride days and weekend evenings are the natural spots.
- **Calories vary a lot:** a light beer is about 100 calories, a regular beer about 150, and a craft IPA or stout 200 to 300. Two IPAs can equal a full meal. If you like craft beer, that's fine, but count it accurately.
- **Why moderation matters for you specifically:** alcohol raises triglycerides, one of the numbers on your cholesterol panel. It also disrupts deep sleep, which slows muscle recovery, and it weakens your resistance to late-night snacking.
- **Practical tips:** have your beer with dinner rather than after it, so it doesn't turn into a snacking session. Drink a glass of water between beers. If you're out with friends, start with a light beer.

### Ice cream

- **Budget:** about ½ to ¾ cup, 2 to 3 times a week. **Always in a bowl, never from the carton.** Eating from the carton is how half a cup becomes two.
- **Know your options:**

| Type | Per ½ cup | Sat fat |
|---|---|---|
| Premium (dense, rich brands) | 250–300 cal | 9–11 g |
| Regular vanilla | about 140 cal | about 4.5 g |
| Light or "slow-churned" | 100–120 cal | about 2 g |
| High-protein light pints | 70–100 cal | about 1–2 g |
| Frozen yogurt or sorbet | 100–130 cal | 0–2 g |

A half cup of premium ice cream can use up two-thirds of your daily saturated fat budget. Light or slow-churned versions taste close to the real thing and are the best everyday choice. Save premium ice cream for an occasional real treat.

- **Make it go further:** top a smaller scoop with a handful of berries or sliced banana.

## 6. Soda and fast food

Linking these two is a smart move. You noticed that soda is your trigger for fast food, so cutting soda breaks the fast-food habit too. A 20-oz soda has about 65 grams of sugar (about 16 teaspoons) and 240 calories. Sugar is also one of the main things that raises triglycerides. And it adds up in dollars: a fountain drink and a fast-food meal together can easily cost $12 to $15. The weekly review will track how much you're saving.

### What to drink instead

- **Sparkling water**, plain or naturally flavored (unsweetened). It gives the carbonation and "something with fizz" feeling, which is often what you're actually craving.
- **Unsweetened iced tea** with lemon. Brew a pitcher at home for almost nothing.
- **Water with lemon, lime, or cucumber.**
- **Coffee**, black or with a splash of milk.
- **Diet soda as an occasional bridge**, if a craving is really bad. It has no sugar, so it's a better choice than regular soda. Keep in mind that your goal is also to break the soda-and-fast-food link, so don't let diet soda become the new trigger.

### Your fast-food escape plan

The real fix is having something faster than the drive-thru already waiting at home. Keep at least two of these ready at all times:

1. **Frozen single portions** of the chili, lentil soup, or beef stew. Microwave for 4 minutes.
2. **A store rotisserie chicken.** Pull the skin off, and pair the meat with a microwave brown rice pouch and a bag of steam-in-the-bag vegetables. Dinner in 5 minutes for a few dollars a serving.
3. **Sandwich makings:** whole-wheat bread, turkey, reduced-fat cheese, tomato.
4. **Canned tuna or salmon** with whole-wheat crackers and an apple.
5. **Eggs** with a slice of toast and some fruit.

### If you do end up at a fast-food place

It happens. When it does, use these rules, and don't feel guilty about it. One meal doesn't break anything.

1. **Water or unsweetened tea to drink.** That's the deal you've made with yourself.
2. **Grilled beats fried.** A grilled chicken sandwich or grilled nuggets instead of crispy.
3. **One patty, not two.** A single burger is fine. Skip the double, the bacon, and the extra cheese.
4. **Small fries or none.** Swap for a side salad or fruit when it's offered.
5. **Skip the shakes.** A medium milkshake can top 600 calories and 20 g of saturated fat.

**Better picks at common chains:**

- **Chick-fil-A:** grilled chicken sandwich, or 12-count grilled nuggets with a side salad or fruit cup.
- **Subway:** 6-inch turkey or chicken on wheat, loaded with vegetables, mustard, and no cheese, or a footlong if it's your whole meal.
- **Chipotle:** burrito bowl with chicken, black beans, fajita vegetables, salsa, and lettuce, with half the rice and no cheese or sour cream. This is close to the chili recipe and gets you about 45 g of protein.
- **Burger places:** a single burger with lettuce, tomato, and mustard, small fries if you must, and water.

## 7. Eating around your training

- **Before the gym or a ride:** carbs plus a little protein, 1 to 2 hours before. Oatmeal, a banana with peanut butter, or toast with eggs. Training on an empty stomach usually means weaker workouts.
- **After the gym or a ride:** protein plus carbs within about 2 hours. A regular meal like chicken and rice is perfect. If dinner is a while away, have a protein shake or chocolate milk to bridge the gap.
- **During long rides (over about 90 minutes):** 30 to 45 g of carbohydrate per hour, about one banana or two fig bars, plus water and electrolytes.

## 9. A simple weekly cooking routine

You don't need to meal prep for hours. One 60- to 90-minute session a week covers most of it:

1. **Start a slow-cooker recipe** in the morning (chili, lentil soup, or beef stew). Rotate between them week to week. That gives you 6 servings, enough for lunches most of the week with a couple left over to freeze.
2. **Cook a batch of lemon-garlic chicken and brown rice** (Recipe 3). That's 4 meals ready to grab.
3. **Mix 2 or 3 jars of overnight oats.**
4. **Wash and cut some vegetables** for snacks.

After a few weeks, your freezer will hold a rotating supply of single portions. On days when you'd normally go through a drive-thru, a homemade meal will be the faster option.
